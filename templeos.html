The museum, on the maker

Terry A. Davis, 1969–2018

One man wrote all of this by himself, and he was not well. Both of those things are simply true, and neither one explains the other away.

Terrence Andrew Davis was born in 1969 in Wisconsin, into a large Catholic family. He took bachelor's and master's degrees in electrical engineering at Arizona State University and went to work as a professional programmer — systems work on Digital's VAX/VMS, including a period at Ticketmaster. He was, before anything else, a competent working engineer.

He was diagnosed with schizophrenia in his twenties, and spoke about it publicly and at length for the rest of his life. He was hospitalised several times. He lost the ability to hold a job.

He began building an operating system around 2003. It passed through several names — J Operating System, then LoseThos, then SparrowOS — before arriving in 2013 at TempleOS, and he released the whole thing into the public domain. He worked on it for roughly fifteen years, alone: a 64-bit kernel, a compiler, a programming language, a filesystem, a document format, a graphics stack and a windowing system, in something like a hundred thousand lines.

What he believed he was building

He believed the system was God's Third Temple, and that its specifications had been given to him: 640×480, sixteen colours, one 8×8 font, one voice of audio, and no networking. He did not regard these as limitations he had accepted. He regarded them as instructions he had followed. The oracle — GodWord, GodDoodle, GodSong, GodBible — was, to him, a working channel: you asked, the machine drew from randomness, and you read what came back.

The exhibit implements all of that exactly as it worked. It neither endorses the claim nor argues with it. Presenting it accurately is the whole job.

His later years

He was often homeless in his last years, living out of a van and then on the street, still writing code and still streaming. He died on 11 August 2018, aged 48, struck by a train in The Dalles, Oregon.

The part that does not reconcile

His public output — streams, forum posts, mailing lists — also included sustained racist abuse. Many of the people who valued his work found that impossible to reconcile with it, and this exhibit does not attempt to reconcile it either. None of it is reproduced here.

Afterwards

TempleOS remains in the public domain and is still studied seriously by operating-system developers, precisely because it is small enough for one person to read all of. Community forks continue the work — ZealOS and Shrine among them — modernising the parts that needed it while keeping the shape of the thing intact.

An idiot admires complexity, a genius admires simplicity. Terry A. Davis

His own framing for what the system was for was that it made a modern machine into a Commodore 64: something you could poke at without asking permission, and start again when you broke it. That is the sentence to hold on to while you use it.

Held the way a museum holds outsider work: with complete respect for the sincerity of the maker, complete honesty about the circumstances, and no condescension in either direction.
The museum, on itself

About this exhibit

Nothing is emulated. There is no x86 core in this page and no disc image. This is a simulation written from scratch in one HTML file, whose job is to let you sit at TempleOS long enough to understand what it was arguing for.

What genuinely works

  • The command line is the compiler. There is no shell language. What you type at the prompt is HolyC, and a real interpreter for a workable subset of it runs in this page. "Hello World\n"; is a complete program. Dir is a function call. Define a function at the prompt and then call it.
  • DolDoc. Every file — source, documentation, and the terminal output you are looking at — is the same rich hypertext format, edited in place. Colours, links, collapsible tree nodes, clickable macros that run HolyC, and sprites embedded inside the text, including inside comments.
  • The sprite in the comment is really in the file. Open a source file, click the picture in the middle of a function, change it in the sprite editor, then run the program. There is no import step because there is no file boundary to cross.
  • Ring 0. The palette, the font and the kernel's own variables are ordinary globals. Assign to them and the screen changes. Overwrite the wrong region and the machine dies and needs rebooting, which takes about a second, as it did.
  • Adam and Seth. A root task that everything descends from, and spawned children with their own windows, in a real (cooperative) scheduler.
  • The oracle. GodWord, GodDoodle, GodSong and GodBible, implemented plainly, with no commentary anywhere inside the system.

What is reconstruction

  • The operating system's source is original. /Kernel, /Compiler and /Adam contain a believable body of HolyC written for this exhibit in the real system's style and vocabulary. It is an illustrative reconstruction, not a transcription — the real code is public domain and widely mirrored, and you should read that if you want the real thing. The system says so itself in /Misc/AboutSource.DD.
  • Every glyph and every sprite in this file was drawn for it. The 8×8 font is original glyph data in the system's style; so is all the artwork.
  • The scripture is a selection. TempleOS shipped the King James Bible, which is public domain. Embedding all of it would be several megabytes, so /Bible carries a substantial selection of passages, and GodBible draws from a larger verse pool. The translation is the KJV throughout; nothing under copyright is included.
  • The key bindings are a coherent reconstruction of the system's own, documented inside the system in /Doc/Keys.DD.
  • Timing is honest but not measured. The real thing compiled faster than this and drew the screen 60 times a second on hardware from 2013.

Constraints that were kept

There is no resolution setting, no colour-depth setting, no font choice, no antialiasing, no memory protection and no networking, because TempleOS had none of those and the absence of each one was a position rather than an oversight. The window scales only by whole pixels. If you go looking for a network configuration panel you will not find one, and the system has a document explaining that you were never going to.

Colophon

One self-contained HTML file. No CDNs, no web fonts, no linked images, no network requests, no build step. Runs from file://. Your files, your sprites, your command history and your window layout persist in LocalStorage; Reinstall in the rail above puts the shipped filesystem back.

A fan-made tribute for educational purposes, in the spirit of the rest of the museum. TempleOS is public domain. The real thing, and the real source, are worth your time.

Guide layer, sound and the CRT glass are museum furniture and can all be switched off. The system underneath does not know they exist.
